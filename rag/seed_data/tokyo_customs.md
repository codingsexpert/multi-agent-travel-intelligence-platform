# Tokyo Cultural Etiquette and Local Customs

Tokyo blends ultra-modern infrastructure with ancient traditions deeply rooted in Shinto and Buddhist philosophy. Understanding local customs ensures respectful and rewarding interactions across the metropolis.

## Tipping Taboo
Tipping is not customary anywhere in Japan. In restaurants, taxis, and ryokans, exceptional hospitality (omotenashi) is considered standard and already reflected in the bill. Leaving extra cash on a table or counter will typically cause confusion or embarrassment, as staff may pursue you down the street thinking you forgot your change.

## Bowing and Greetings
A slight bow (ojigi) is the traditional manner of greeting, saying thank you, or expressing apology. A 15-to-30 degree bow is polite for travelers. Handshakes are understood by many in hospitality, but a bow accompanied by a polite "Arigato gozaimasu" (Thank you very much) is warmly appreciated.

## Shoes and Footwear Etiquette
Always remove your outdoor shoes when stepping onto tatami matting, entering traditional ryokans, temple halls, tea houses, and certain dining establishments with sunken kotatsu seating. Slippers are typically provided at the genkan (entryway). Remember to swap slippers for designated restroom slippers when using the washroom.

## Public Transit and Commuter Manners
Tokyo's train and subway network (JR East, Tokyo Metro, Toei) carries millions daily with clockwork quietness. Talking on mobile phones inside train carriages is strictly forbidden. Set devices to silent ("manner mode") and keep conversational voices low. During morning rush hours, designated women-only carriages are operated on major lines.

## Trash and Waste Management
Public trash bins are extremely rare across Japanese cities due to historical safety protocols and recycling culture. Travelers should always carry a small plastic bag in their daypack to retain personal trash until returning to their hotel or using recycling receptacles located next to public beverage vending machines.
